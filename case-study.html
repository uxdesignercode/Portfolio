Overview

Homesfy is a B2B and B2C real estate platform connecting property buyers with transparent property data. Our mission: simplify property discovery and evaluation through comprehensive, accessible information.

As the lead designer, I led research, designed the portal interface, and validated usability with real buyers. This case study captures how we improved property discovery through intentional design decisions rooted in user research and business strategy.

Core Problems

  • Complex pricing model — Buyers confused by multiple variables
  • Poor search filtering — Users struggled to narrow results
  • Inconsistent property display — Information scattered across pages
  • Lack of transparency — Trust issues due to incomplete data

We analyzed 3 leading real estate platforms to understand competitive patterns and identify gaps in the market.

Problem: Information Overload

Buyers faced cluttered interfaces with too many features. Key property data was buried beneath layers of navigation, making quick comparisons impossible.

Solution: Progressive Disclosure

We designed a card-based layout that surfaces high-priority information first — location, price, key features — with expandable sections for deeper analysis. This respects the buyer's cognitive load.

Problem: Low Trust in Listings

Incomplete data and hidden costs eroded user confidence. Buyers didn't know if a listing was truly "move-in ready" or required work.

Solution: Transparent Data Model

Every property now shows verified data badges, cost breakdowns, and condition ratings. Clear labels indicate data freshness and what information is missing, turning transparency into a competitive advantage.

What I Learned

  1. Transparency builds trust faster than polish. Users valued seeing what data was missing over a fully polished interface with hidden gaps.
  2. Progressive disclosure respects user agency. Not every user wants every detail upfront — giving them control over information depth reduced cognitive load.
  3. Real user testing catches what analytics hide. Watching buyers struggle with filters revealed deeper mental models we hadn't anticipated in design.
  4. Iteration compounds learning. Each test round informed the next design cycle, and small changes (like filter labels) had outsized impact on usability.
